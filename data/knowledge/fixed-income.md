# Understanding Fixed Income

## What Is Fixed Income?
Fixed income investments, commonly known as bonds, represent loans made by an investor to a borrower. The borrower is typically a government entity or a corporation that needs capital to finance public projects, maintain infrastructure, or expand business operations. When you purchase a bond, the issuer promises to pay you regular interest payments over a specified period and return the principal loan amount when the bond reaches maturity. Investors who prefer steady cash flow often hold fixed income assets. In a diversified portfolio, these assets can be held directly or through pooled funds. A simulated example is the Delta Income Bond Fund (DIBF), which holds a collection of bonds from various issuers.

## How Fixed Income Gains or Loses Value
Although bonds are designed to pay regular interest, their market prices fluctuate before maturity. The primary driver of bond prices in the open market is the movement of general interest rates. When newly issued bonds offer higher interest rates than older bonds, the older bonds become less attractive, causing their market value to drop. On the other hand, when market interest rates decrease, existing bonds with higher interest payments become more valuable, lifting their market price. Additionally, changes in the perceived financial health of the borrower can affect prices. If an issuer demonstrates stronger financial stability, demand for its debt can rise.

## Interest Rate Risk and Credit Risk
A central risk in fixed income investing is interest rate risk. When broader interest rates rise across the economy, the resale value of existing bonds declines. This is especially true for bonds with longer maturities, because their fixed payments are locked in for a longer duration while higher rates become available elsewhere. Another key consideration is credit risk, which is the possibility that a borrower may experience financial distress and fail to make scheduled interest payments or repay the principal. Even funds holding multiple bonds, like DIBF, can experience fluctuations in value as market interest rates shift.
